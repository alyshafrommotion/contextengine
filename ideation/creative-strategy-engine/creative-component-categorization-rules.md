# Creative Component Categorization Rules

This is the working reference for how to sort anything the user shares into the component libraries. When the user gives a video or ad and says what they like about it, the liked aspects get classified here — sometimes into one library, sometimes into several. Read this before saving something new, and update it whenever a real save teaches a new distinction.

**What the libraries hold:** reusable, brand-agnostic raw material. Every component saved to a library must pass one test — could this be lifted out of the source and redeployed for a different brand, product, or niche? Anything brand-specific belongs in that brand's working files, not in a library. For examples of what belongs in any library, look at the components already saved in it.

## The component types

### Messaging Strategy
The kind of argument an ad makes — the reusable persuasive stance, independent of any brand. Confronting the audience with the inadequacy of their current solution is a messaging strategy. The brand-specific expression of a strategy (the strategy applied to one offer, for one persona) is a **messaging angle**, and angles are NOT saved to the library — they live in the brand's working files. The library stores the strategy underneath.

**Test:** is the thing being pointed out the *stance or argument type* — what kind of case the ad is making for why the audience should care — rather than the words, the structure, or the look? Strip the brand and product out; if a reusable way-of-arguing remains, that's the strategy. If nothing remains without the brand, it was an angle, not a strategy — extract the strategy underneath it and save that instead.

### Visual Format
The container the ad comes in: the recognizable shape of the video or static, separate from what it says. How it's shot, laid out, or structured.

**Test:** if you stripped out the specific words and the specific persuasion technique, what's left is the *shape* of the content. Would this shape work with completely different copy and a completely different argument? Then it's a format.

### Creative Mechanic
The underlying structural or cognitive device that makes the piece work: how the ad constructs meaning between its hook, visuals, and narrative — a reveal structure, a proof device, a comparison move, a reusable sentence template.

**Test:** if you swapped out the specific subject but kept the *underlying move*, would it still work the same way in a totally different niche? Mechanics are about how the persuasion or humor actually functions, separate from both the visual shape (format) and the argument being made (strategy).

**Distinct from strategy:** the strategy is *what kind of case* the ad makes; the mechanic is *the device that delivers it*. One strategy can be executed through many mechanics, and one mechanic can serve many strategies.

### Hook
The opening line, visual, or moment of a video ad, meant to stop the scroll and earn the next few seconds of attention.

**The test: would this sound natural if a person spoke it out loud at the very start of a video?** A hook is spoken-shaped. It reads like something a person would actually say, not a written line dressed up as speech. It can be a spoken line, a visual pattern-interrupt, a question, or a natural-sounding stat or claim delivered out loud.

### Headline
The primary written line of an ad, or an on-screen title/text overlay used *in place of* a spoken hook.

**The test: would this sound unnatural or stiff if spoken out loud, but work well written down?** Headlines are written-shaped. A headline can appear inside a video as on-screen text when the creator doesn't speak the line.

**Hook vs. headline default:** on-screen text overlay with no spoken line is a headline; a line the creator says out loud, or clearly could say out loud unchanged, is a hook. Do not rephrase or soften a written line to force it into sounding "spoken" just to justify a hook entry — if it needs rephrasing to sound natural, it's a headline.

**Save patterns, not lines.** Hook and headline entries are saved as reusable *patterns* — the structure of how the line works ([statement that triggers an expectation] + [reality that contradicts it]) — never as verbatim lines to reuse. The specific line from the source is logged inside the entry as one example fill of the pattern.

**Mark the wording.** Every language-based entry (hook, headline, or sentence-template mechanic) records a **Wording** field with one of three values. *Fixed phrase*: the source's literal words are the asset — reuse them exactly and only write the fill. *Skeleton*: the frame is reusable but every slot gets rewritten — no fixed catchphrase carries it. *Structure only*: the source's words are welded to their specific use case — never reuse them; only the shape travels. Never assume; decide per entry.

## Multi-library saves

One shared video often carries more than one thing worth keeping — a format worth saving AND a mechanic inside it AND a line strong enough to stand alone. Save each liked aspect to its own library and cross-reference the entries back to each other and to the shared source. A mechanic that is a reusable sentence template (the recipe) can also produce a hook or headline entry (the finished line) when the specific line stands on its own; save both, cross-referenced.

Never force a save into one library when two aspects were genuinely liked — and never pad into extra libraries when only one thing was actually pointed out. Save what was liked, nothing more.

## Library indexes

Each library folder keeps an `index.md` — a table of contents with one row per entry and a one-line description. The elaboration, reasoning, and source-link examples live in the individual entry files. Every save updates two things: the new entry file, and its row in that library's index.

## The trends exception

A trend is a time-bound viral package tied to a specific cultural moment — its elements are one inseparable unit, and reusing it means participating in the trend as it currently exists, not extracting a timeless structure. A trend can live in any library: a visual format trend, a hook trend, a phrase-plus-visual package. Wherever it lands, it's saved as one unit in a `trends/` subfolder of that library, and its elements are never split out into separate entries in other libraries.

**Trend status is never inferred or assumed. Something is only filed as a trend when the user explicitly says it's a trend.** If they don't say it, classify it through the normal libraries — even if it looks culturally current.

## Decision order when something new comes in

1. Did the user explicitly say this is a trend? If yes, save it as one unit in the `trends/` subfolder of whichever library it most naturally belongs to; do not split its elements into other libraries. If they didn't say it, it is not a trend — continue.
2. Is the liked aspect the *shape* of the content, independent of words or argument? → visual format.
3. Is the liked aspect *how the persuasion or humor works* — the device, the move, the template? → creative mechanic.
4. Is the liked aspect the *kind of argument* being made, strippable from this brand and reusable as a stance? → messaging strategy. (If it only exists with the brand attached, it's an angle — extract and save the strategy underneath.)
5. Is there also a specific line that stands on its own? Spoken-shaped → hook. Written-shaped → headline. Cross-reference back to any related mechanic/format entry.
6. Multiple aspects liked → multiple saves, cross-referenced. One aspect liked → one save.
7. When genuinely unsure between two libraries, save to the one matching the *primary* thing the user pointed out, and note the borderline call inside the entry rather than guessing silently.
